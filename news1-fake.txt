OUTRAGE! Opposition Holds “FAKE PARLIAMENT” in Shocking Midnight Drama, Ruling Party Calls It “Greatest Betrayal in History”

Insiders claim “nothing like this has EVER happened.” Experts “refuse to comment” but “know the truth.” What really took place behind closed doors will leave you SPEECHLESS.

The Night the Nation GASPED

In a scene that left the entire country STUNNED, leaders of the Samyukt Jan Morcha (SJM) allegedly gathered at a secret location in the capital and staged what sources are calling a “so-called trial” of Chief Electoral Officer Vikrant Mahale. Nobody was prepared for what followed.

According to “people familiar with the matter,” who requested anonymity because they were “afraid of what might happen to them,” the gathering began late in the evening. It continued, they claim, “well past the hour when honest citizens are asleep.” One unnamed attendee described the atmosphere as “electric,” “tense” and “unlike anything I have witnessed in forty years.” When pressed for details, the attendee “could not remember” which year the forty years began.

The Rashtriya Vikas Dal (RVD), the ruling party, wasted no time. Within hours, its leaders were demanding answers. By morning, the whole country was talking about it. Or so we are told.

“This is a MOCKERY,” thundered RVD spokesperson Ramesh Dhanvani at a packed press conference, though the exact number of people present remains “disputed” and “possibly exaggerated.” “They are DESTROYING democracy FOREVER. LIES, LIES and more LIES. History will not forgive them.”

Whether history will forgive anyone remains unconfirmed.

A Setting “Straight Out of a Movie”

Eyewitnesses, none of whom could be independently verified, described the venue as “eerily resembling” a real parliamentary chamber. Rows of chairs were arranged in a semicircle. A raised seat, said to be the “Speaker’s chair,” stood at the front. Microphones were reportedly placed at “strategic positions,” though what makes a position strategic was not explained.

“It looked EXACTLY like the real thing,” said one man who claimed to have walked past the building “by pure coincidence” at an hour when nobody walks past anything. “I saw the lights. I heard the shouting. I knew something BIG was happening.”

The venue, identified in some reports as Harshad Bhavan, the residence of SJM president Anand Deshmukh, was allegedly “transformed overnight” into a “shadow chamber.” Asked how a residence could be transformed overnight, the source said, “Money. Lots of money. Don’t ask me how I know.”

A second source, who described himself as “a friend of a friend of someone who works nearby,” said the building’s lawn was “swarming” with people. He estimated the crowd at “between fifty and fifty thousand,” a range he called “my honest assessment.”

An RVD insider claimed the opposition had “even printed fake order papers to make the whole thing look real.” A copy of these papers has not been seen by this publication, but the insider insisted they exist “in a drawer somewhere.”

Who Presided? The Answer Will SHOCK You

The proceedings were allegedly presided over by former Speaker of the lower house Sulochana Rane, a veteran politician known for her “iron will and sharp tongue,” according to people who have either praised or feared her.

Rane, wearing what one onlooker called “a stern expression that said everything,” reportedly opened the session with a statement that “sent shivers down the spine of every person present.” The exact words are unclear, but at least three attendees agree they were “very serious.”

“She did not smile once,” said one witness. “Not ONCE. That is how you know it was serious.”

A rival camp has alleged that Rane “enjoyed every second of the drama” and had “been rehearsing her role for weeks.” Rane’s office has not responded to these claims. This publication takes that silence to be “extremely telling.”

The So-Called “Charges”

According to documents “leaked to select journalists” (and by “select” we mean “the ones who agreed to run this story”), the SJM leaders listed a series of “grave allegations” against Chief Electoral Officer Mahale. Among them:

That the Special Roll Cleansing Drive (SRCD), a nationwide exercise to update the voter lists, was “conducted with sinister intent.”
That millions of names were “mysteriously removed” from voter lists, “possibly by accident, possibly not.”
That the Electoral Commission had become “a puppet of those in power.”
That Mahale had “refused to answer simple questions asked by honest citizens.”
That the entire process was “a trap.”

A trap for whom was not specified.

The Electoral Commission has called the allegations “baseless.” However, a “highly placed source” within the commission, who “wishes to remain nameless for obvious reasons,” allegedly admitted privately that “there may have been a few issues.” What issues, how many, and how serious were “not discussed.” But the source’s tone was described as “worried.”

“NOTHING BUT A PUPPET”: The Quote That Went VIRAL

The most explosive moment of the night, according to multiple unconfirmed accounts, came when SJM leader Kishore Tandel rose to speak. The room fell silent. Even the ceiling fan, according to one dramatic account, “seemed to stop.”

“The man at the top of the opposition is nothing but a puppet,” Tandel reportedly declared, pointing a finger at nobody in particular, though several sources say he pointed “at the sky” and others say “at the carpet.”

The statement has since gone VIRAL, racking up what some have called “millions” of views, though no one has verified the number. A viral clip allegedly shows the moment, but analysts say the video is “grainy,” “poorly lit,” and “possibly of a different event entirely.”

Tandel, reached for comment, said he “stands by every word he may or may not have said.” His spokesperson later clarified that Tandel “was misquoted,” “quoted accurately but out of context,” and “never said any of it,” in that order.

RVD Hits Back: “The Greatest Betrayal in History”

If the opposition thought their midnight drama would go unanswered, they were badly mistaken.

At a hastily arranged press conference at the RVD headquarters, party spokespersons lined up one after another to condemn the event. Ramesh Dhanvani, visibly “shaking with rage” according to an aide, called it “the greatest betrayal in the history of this country.”

“They hold a MOCK parliament,” Dhanvani said, his voice rising. “Next they will hold a mock election. Then a mock government. Then a mock country!”

The crowd of journalists reportedly “gasped” at the phrase “mock country.” It is unclear what a mock country would look like or whether one is currently being planned.

A second RVD leader, Pushpa Garodia, went further. “The Samyukt Jan Morcha is spreading flames of anarchy,” she said, using a phrase that “has been trending ever since.” She added that the opposition should “keep their leader as their mock prime minister for life.” The crowd, said an eyewitness, “erupted,” though whether in applause or confusion was “hard to tell.”

Garodia also claimed that “the people of this nation have seen through the drama.” When asked how she knew what the people had seen, she said, “I can feel it.”

The “Statistics War” Nobody Asked For

In a twist that “stunned” political observers, the RVD pivoted from attacking the opposition’s event to presenting what they called “hard, undeniable statistics” from the Institute for Global Prosperity Metrics (IGPM), a respected international body, or so the RVD says.

According to the RVD, the IGPM report shows that extreme poverty in the country has “fallen dramatically,” “faster than anyone predicted,” and “to levels that make the opposition’s complaints look ridiculous.”

“The IGPM’s cold, hard numbers have in a way mocked their mock parliament,” said RVD spokesperson Sudarshan Pethkar, delighted by his own wordplay. He repeated it twice for the cameras. Nobody laughed, but several reporters “nodded politely.”

The actual figures were not read out at the press conference. Asked why, Pethkar said, “They are very large numbers. Too large to read.”

Independent economists, who “did not want to be named because they have mortgages,” told this publication that the IGPM report “may or may not say that.” One economist claimed to have “skimmed it once on a train.” Another said it “seemed fine.” A third said, “I have strong feelings about it, but I would rather not share them.”

The SJM, for its part, called the report “cooked,” “fabricated” and “obviously written by someone who has never been poor.” They did not provide an alternative report. Asked whether they had read the one in question, a spokesperson said, “We don’t need to read it. We know what it says.”

“Vote Chori”: The Accusation That Refuses to Die

No political drama is complete without a scandal from the distant past. Sure enough, the RVD dug one up.

In a stunning claim that “rewrote history,” senior RVD leader Dhruv Karnik alleged that the first prime minister of the country, the late Bhairav Kulkarni, “became prime minister by vote chori,” or vote theft.

“It was Bhairav Kulkarni who stole the vote of the great Sardar Madhavrao Ghatge,” Karnik declared, “and in doing so, stole the destiny of a nation.”

Historians, when contacted, were “baffled.” One professor, who asked not to be named “for fear of academic reprisal,” said the claim “has no evidence.” Another said, “I’d have to check.” A third said, “Is this a trick question?”

The SJM dismissed the allegation as “pathetic,” “desperate” and “a distraction.” But RVD leaders insisted it was “proof” that “the opposition has no right to lecture anyone about elections.” How a decades-old claim justifies or invalidates a present-day argument was not explained, and no one in the room appeared to ask.

The Woman Who “Knew Everything”

One of the strangest figures to emerge from the night was a woman identified only as “Mrs. K,” who allegedly worked “in a support capacity” at the venue.

“I saw it all,” Mrs. K told reporters, her voice dropping to a whisper. “The speeches. The documents. The tea.”

“The tea?” asked a reporter.

“The tea was cold,” she said ominously. “That is all I will say.”

Mrs. K has since declined further interviews. Her exact role, her full name and whether she exists remain “unverified.” But the phrase “cold tea” has become a meme, with thousands of users reportedly posting images of lukewarm beverages captioned “The tea was cold. That is all I will say.”

Political analysts say the cold tea “may be a metaphor.” For what, they cannot say.

Experts Weigh In (Sort Of)

As the controversy raged, a parade of “experts” appeared on television to share their views. Their level of insight varied.

Dr. Ashwin Pawar, a self-described “political scientist and part-time astrologer,” said the event was “a clear sign of deep unrest” and “something the stars predicted.” He declined to specify which stars.

Retired bureaucrat Mohan Sathe said, “In my day, this would never have happened,” a statement he has reportedly made about “almost everything.”

Media commentator Reshma Joglekar called the proceedings “a masterclass in political theatre.” Another panellist, speaking just after her, called them “a masterclass in political incompetence.” The host concluded, “So we are all agreed.”

A so-called “constitutional expert,” whose credentials “could not be confirmed,” argued the whole thing was “technically legal,” “technically illegal” and “technically irrelevant,” depending on “how you look at it.” He then ran out of time.

Social Media Erupts

If the real-world reaction was heated, the online reaction was nuclear.

Within an hour of the first reports, the hashtags #MockParliament, #PuppetGate and #ColdTea were all trending. One user wrote, “Is this real life?” Another replied, “No idea, but I’m here for it.” A third posted a photo of a parrot with the caption “This parrot has more integrity than the entire political class.” The post was shared “over a million times,” though the count has since been revised to “several thousand.”

Supporters of the SJM praised the event as “bold” and “necessary.” RVD supporters called it “treason” and “a joke,” often in the same sentence. A small but vocal group of neutral users posted memes about “politicians being politicians.”

Fact-checkers struggled to keep up. One fact-checking account tweeted, “We are looking into it.” Twelve hours later it tweeted, “We are still looking into it.” At the time of writing, it has gone silent.

The Electoral Commission Responds

For its part, the Electoral Commission issued a carefully worded statement, which sources describe as “calm, measured and slightly annoyed.”

“The Commission has noted the events of the previous night with concern,” it read. “The Commission has conducted every exercise in accordance with law, procedure and tradition. The Commission does not respond to theatrical performances.”

The statement did not mention Mahale by name. It also did not mention the SRCD, the voter lists, the allegations or the alleged trial. Critics called it “evasive.” Supporters called it “dignified.” One commentator called it “a masterclass in saying nothing.”

Mahale himself has remained silent, though a “close aide” claimed he “watched the whole thing on television with a cup of tea.” The aide did not specify whether the tea was cold.

What Was Actually Decided?

Despite the pomp, it remains entirely unclear what the “mock parliament” actually decided. Some say a “resolution” was passed. Others say “a declaration.” A third group insists “a poem was read.”

According to one account, the participants “voted unanimously” on a “symbolic motion” that Mahale “had lost the trust of the people.” Another account says the vote was “split,” with “at least three people abstaining due to hunger.” A third says no vote was taken at all.

Legal experts say a symbolic trial has “no legal force whatsoever,” a point the SJM does not dispute. Their argument, according to leaders, is that “the symbolism is the point.” The RVD retorts that “symbolism without substance is just noise.” The two sides then, in the words of one reporter, “stared at each other for a very long time.”

Behind the Scenes: The Rehearsals

In a development that has “raised eyebrows,” a source claims the opposition held “at least three rehearsals” before the main event.

“They practised the entire thing,” said the source, who claims to have been “briefly in the next room.” “Who would stand when. Who would shout when. Who would look sad when.”

If true, the claim suggests the drama was “carefully choreographed.” The SJM denies this, saying the event was “spontaneous and from the heart.” When asked how a spontaneous event could involve printed order papers and a presiding officer, a spokesperson said, “We are very organised people.”

An RVD insider offered a rival theory: that the entire event was “an elaborate distraction” from “something else,” though the insider could not say what. “That is the beauty of a distraction,” he explained. “You never know what it is distracting from.”

The Sunday Rally: “A Storm Is Coming”

All eyes now turn to Sunday, when the SJM is scheduled to hold a mega rally at the Maidan Ground, which organisers say will be “the largest gathering in the history of the republic.” Previous gatherings have also been described this way, but organisers insist “this time it is true.”

Posters reportedly show the slogan “Save the Vote, Save the Nation.” Rumours suggest “surprise guests,” “special announcements” and “a shocking reveal” that “will change politics forever.” Organisers declined to confirm any of it, saying only that “you will have to be there.”

The RVD has called the rally “a desperate attempt to stay relevant.” It has also, in a move critics call “contradictory,” announced plans for “a counter-event” the same day, to “set the record straight.” Asked how a desperate and irrelevant event could require a counter-event, a spokesperson said, “Do not confuse the issue.”

Authorities have announced “heightened security arrangements,” including “barricades, drones and, if necessary, more barricades.” Traffic in the capital is expected to be “chaotic,” “extremely chaotic” or “somewhat chaotic,” depending on whom you ask.

Citizens Speak Out

To gauge public opinion, this publication spoke to ordinary citizens at a tea stall near the capital’s central market. The results were “mixed,” “inconclusive” and “mostly about the weather.”

“I don’t understand any of it,” said a vegetable seller. “But I know prices are high.”

“They are all the same,” said a retired schoolteacher, a view he said he has held “for thirty years.”

“I think it’s good that people are talking,” said a college student. “I also think it’s bad that people are talking. I am confused.”

One auto-rickshaw driver offered the sharpest assessment: “Whoever wins, I still have to drive. So I will drive.”

No one at the tea stall could name the Chief Electoral Officer. One person guessed “Mahesh,” and another “Mukund.” Neither was right, though both were “close in spirit.”

A Nation Divided, Or Possibly Just Distracted

Analysts say the controversy highlights “deep fractures” in the political landscape. Others say it is “business as usual.” A third group says it is “business as usual with better lighting.”

“There is a pattern,” said one columnist, her voice grave. “Every few months, something happens. Everyone is outraged. Then something else happens. And everyone forgets.” She paused. “Or possibly they do not forget. I haven’t checked.”

Pollsters are scrambling to gauge public sentiment. An early survey of “a representative sample” of 214 people found that “47 percent are concerned,” “46 percent are not concerned” and “7 percent did not understand the question.” The margin of error was not disclosed. The sample’s representativeness was not explained. But the poll has been widely shared “because it has a pie chart.”
